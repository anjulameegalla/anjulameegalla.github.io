Beginners Guide to Social Engineering: Part 1

Posted on 2026-10-16 Reading time ≈ 4 mins.

Picture this. Your phone rings and a friendly voice says, “Hi, this is Dilan from IT Support. We’ve noticed some unusual activity on your account. I’ll need the code we just texted you, or your account will be locked in ten minutes.”

You’re a bit worried and you don’t want to lose access, so you read the code out. Nothing was hacked. No virus, no clever software exploit. You simply handed over the keys yourself.

That is social engineering, and it’s one of the most common ways people and organizations get compromised.

So what is social engineering?

Social engineering is the practice of manipulating people into giving up information, access, or money by playing on human psychology instead of breaking into a system with technical tricks.

Think of a castle with huge walls, deep moats and strong gates. Those are your firewalls, antivirus and encryption. A social-engineer doesn’t try to break down the walls. They walk up to the guard, smile, say they’re here to deliver something for the king, and the guard opens the gate. The strongest security in the world doesn’t help much if someone can talk their way past it.

This matters more than most beginners expect. Industry reports like the Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report 1 regularly find that the human element plays a role in a majority of breaches. Attackers are practical. They go after whatever is easiest, and very often that’s a person rather than a machine.

Why does it work on smart people?

A common myth is that only careless or non-technical people fall for these tricks. That isn’t true. Social engineering works because it targets how all human brains work and intelligence doesn’t switch those instincts off.

Attackers rely on a handful of predictable triggers. The first is authority. We’re wired to listen to bosses, banks, police, and IT departments, so attackers pretend to be them. The second is urgency. When we’re rushed, we stop thinking carefully, which is why messages are full of lines like “act now” or “your account closes today.”

Then there’s fear, such as “suspicious login detected!”, which pushes us to react before we reason. On the flip side, greed and curiosity work just as well. A prize, a discount, or “see who viewed your profile” is hard to ignore. Attackers also use trust and likability. Someone friendly and helpful is easy to say yes to. A related trick is reciprocity; they do you a small favor first, like “fixing” a problem for you, so you feel you owe them one. Finally there’s social proof, where they imply everyone else has already done it (“all staff have completed this form”).

The most useful thing to remember here is this: if a message makes you feel rushed, scared or unusually excited, pause. That feeling is usually the attack working.

How an attack usually unfolds

Most social engineering attacks follow the same basic pattern, whether it’s a phone call, an email, or someone standing at your office door.

It starts with research. The attacker learns about their target using public information: social media profiles, the company website, job listings, even photos. The more they know, the more believable they sound. Next comes the hook. They build a story, called a pretext, and make contact. This might be a fake email from “HR”, a call from “the bank”, or a message from a “new colleague” who needs help.

Then comes the exploit, where the attacker gets what they came for. You click the link, share the password, approve the payment, or hold the door open. Finally, there’s the exit. They disappear, often deleting traces, and by the time you realize something is wrong, they’re long gone.

It’s not just theory: real examples

In July 2020, Twitter had one of its biggest security incidents. Attackers didn’t hack Twitter’s servers directly. Twitter said they ran a phone spear-phishing attack on a small number of employees2. That access was then used to take over high-profile accounts and run a Bitcoin scam. It was a human trick, not a technical one.

Another well-known example is Business Email Compromise (BEC). An attacker poses as a CEO or supplier and emails someone in finance with an “urgent” request to transfer money. It sounds simple, but the FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center reported about $2.77 billion in BEC losses for 2024 alone3.

It’s also worth knowing about Kevin Mitnick, a famous former hacker who later became a security consultant. He often argued that people are the easiest way into any system. His book The Art of Deception is a great, readable follow-up if this topic interests you.

Wrapping up..

To sum up, social engineering targets people, not computers. It works by pulling on emotions like fear, urgency, trust, and curiosity. And most attacks follow the same simple pattern: research, hook, exploit, exit.
